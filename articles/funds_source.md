# Mutual Funds and Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)

**Source:** [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/characteristics-mutual-funds-exchange-traded-funds)  
**Source date:** April 29, 2025  
**Reviewed:** November 3, 2026  
**Audience:** People learning the basics of pooled investment funds

## What they have in common

Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) pool money from multiple investors and invest it in stocks, bonds, other assets, or a combination. A share represents a proportional ownership interest in the fund portfolio and its gains or losses. Both types can be actively managed or designed to follow an index, and both can provide diversification. Neither type guarantees that an investor will make money or prevents losses. A fund's actual holdings and strategy determine how diversified it is.

## Main difference: how shares trade and are priced

Investors buy mutual fund shares from the fund or through an intermediary and can redeem them with the fund. Orders are priced at the fund's next calculated net asset value (NAV) per share, typically calculated once at the end of each business day. An order entered during the day therefore does not have a known final share price until that NAV is calculated.

Retail investors buy and sell ETF shares in market transactions on a national stock exchange, so a brokerage account is needed. ETF shares trade at market prices during the trading day. The market price can be higher or lower than the ETF's NAV (a premium or discount) and can change while the market is open.

## Fees and taxes

Both fund types have fees and expenses, and those costs reduce investment returns. The exact costs depend on the fund and how it is bought or held; review the prospectus and brokerage charges rather than assuming one type is always cheaper.

In taxable accounts, investors may owe tax on capital-gains distributions from either type of fund. Many ETFs have historically had fewer such distributions than similar mutual funds because of how ETF portfolios may be managed, but this is not guaranteed. The SEC bulletin says there is no tax difference between an ETF and a mutual fund held in a tax-advantaged account such as a 401(k) or IRA.

## Practical comparison

| Feature | Mutual fund | ETF |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where retail shares are bought or sold | From the fund or through an intermediary; shares can be redeemed with the fund | On an exchange through a brokerage account |
| When the share price is set | At the next calculated NAV, typically once at the end of the business day | At the market price throughout the trading day |
| Price versus NAV | Transactions use the next calculated NAV, subject to applicable fees | Market price can be above or below NAV |
| Investment approach | Can be active or index-based | Can be active or index-based |

These are structural differences, not a recommendation that one type is better. Compare the specific fund's objective, holdings, risks, fees, and account context.
